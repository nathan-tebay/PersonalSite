The Senior Engineer Paradox

I see posts about Senior Engineers interviewing like they are Junior, with various explanations. Here's my take.

Nathan Tebay · April 2026

I've been hesitant to call myself a Senior, not because I wasn't doing the work, but because "Senior" is largely a matter of environment.

Most of my career has been at smaller companies: one with three developers, where I was the only one without a split role; a couple in the 300-person range; one two-man startup; and an ill-fated stint in Oracle consulting that came through an acquisition.

The only place where I really had a well-defined lane was the smallest company. I was full-stack, from database to front end and everything in between. I came in green and learned quickly. I took ownership of my projects and worked with everyone in the company to make the best product we could. Sometimes I wonder why I left that role. I know better than to romanticize the past, but time has a way of making you question things.

In the Oracle role, my title was Principal Technical Consultant. At the time, I didn't even know what my high-level title really meant, and the benefits certainly didn't make it any clearer.

Across all of these roles, the common thread was a get-shit-done attitude. I can't say I was always happy about it, but I put my head down and did the work. I was disappointed when colleagues moved on to "better" things, but I only really cared when I ended up taking on their workload without any of the reward. That was especially true at Oracle, and it's a big part of why I'm not particularly interested in big tech roles.

Over the course of my career, I've checked all the Senior boxes: design ownership, mentoring, stakeholder communication, process improvement. Often I've done all of those things in the same role, even if not all at the same time. Those experiences became tools I learned to use. Some are rustier than others, and some are better suited to specific situations, but they're there.

Most recently, I worked on CI/CD and Kubernetes, partnering with software developers and IT on implementation and adoption. Three years ago, I was writing a lot of Python, Groovy, and Bash. Before that, I worked in Node-RED with JavaScript and Bootstrap. Before that, it was C# and VB.NET in .NET environments.

These are all tools I can polish back up and put to work quickly. But when you're job hunting, you may be asked about any of them in a given interview.

One week, you know you have a technical interview for a backend role using Python, so you're brushing up on Python while also thinking about system design, load balancing, and database schema. Three days later, the next interview is more DevOps-focused: CI/CD, Argo CD, Rancher, AWS. These are all things you've touched, some in depth, but not all recently.

That's part of why Senior developers often struggle in interviews: the scope is both broad and deep.

And that's just the technical side. On top of that, you're also trying to be personable, to help the interviewers get to know you while learning about them and the role. There's never unlimited time, of course, but often the process feels far too rushed to meaningfully show what you bring.

As a Junior in many environments, your job is to write code based on the ticket in front of you. When you get stuck, you reach for a Senior. Seniors review and merge your code, resolve merge conflicts, and talk to stakeholders when something goes sideways.

But in interviews, the Senior may be asked to reverse a linked list in Python while competing against someone who has spent the last three years doing nothing but writing Python, and who isn't going home so mentally fried that they can't spend 30 minutes on a few LeetCode problems.

Of course they may "perform" better in interviews. But often that's a performance without the depth that comes from experience.

If they notice a hole in the requirements, they're going above and beyond. As a Senior, you're expected to be responsible for noticing that hole in the first place.

I keep seeing recruiters on LinkedIn asking why Senior candidates often perform poorly in interviews. To me, the answer is pretty obvious.

In large organizations, Seniors may be specialized enough to focus on coding, mentoring, process improvement, or stakeholder feedback without any one of those responsibilities consuming most of their time.

In smaller organizations, that's rarely the case. You wear many hats, and every time you take one off, there's at least one more waiting to replace it. It becomes very hard not to be a generalist. Yet job postings are usually written to find specialists, and in my experience, many Seniors simply haven't had the luxury of becoming one.

Too often, you walk into an interview blind: not sure of the coding environment, sometimes not even sure of the language. And you can't say, "Let me look that up and make sure I'm on the right page," even though that's exactly what many Seniors do every day while working across multiple technologies. Even in languages you know well, context switching is real and constant.

I'm not saying I know the solution. But I've often thought about saying, "Give me a month and I'll show you." Because I know I can. I've done it before.
