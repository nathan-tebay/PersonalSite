Engineering culture · Personal experience

I didn't leave because of a bad boss or a bad review. I left because I stopped believing the company cared about the same things I did.

Nathan Tebay · May 2026

Quitting doesn't happen overnight. It builds up over time, with small frustrations, ignored requests, and clear signs the company doesn't care about what matters to you.

Here's my story. I spent a few years as a senior software engineer at a fintech company. You can probably guess which one, but that's not important. What matters is the pattern. I'm writing this to be honest about what happened, including my own mistakes, because I think other engineers will see parts of their own experience here.

The Kubernetes trap

After two years of running Kubernetes clusters in production, I was the only one who could support them. I didn't plan it that way. It just happened because nobody stepped in.

I wrote docs. I ran cross-training. I spent months meeting with IT, trying to hand things off. They were always too busy putting out fires. Management agreed it was a problem, but nothing changed.

The more I kept things running, the more the company leaned on me. My reliability just hid the real problems.

The breaking point came in March. My parents planned a big family cruise, something we'd saved for and looked forward to. The company asked me to bring my laptop so I could support a production install during the trip. I said no. The problem wasn't the timing. The problem was they'd had two years to make sure I wasn't the only one who could do this work. They didn't. I was still the single point of failure, and it was somehow my job to work around that on my own time.

At Christmas, my director asked me in front of everyone if I was taking my laptop on vacation, then laughed and said he was joking. It was supposed to be funny, but it just showed he missed the point. The issue wasn't about carrying a laptop. It was that after two years, nobody had figured out what to do when I wasn't around. Instead of fixing it, they made it a joke.

Year 1
Writing Python, still having fun. New role, interesting problems, building things that matter. This is what software engineering is supposed to feel like.
Year 2–3
Kubernetes ownership gap emerges. Documentation written, cross-training attempted, IT meetings ongoing. No handoff achieved.
Year 3
Laptop request submitted for hardware capable of running Docker Desktop. Sits unanswered for over a year.
Year 3–4
AI tooling deliberation begins. Seven months of director-level debate. The owner finally intervenes. Result: a ChatGPT Enterprise chatbot with no API, no Copilot, no code completion.
Year 4
Dev environment initiative stalls. Full leadership buy-in secured, then six months of no movement. The next step would have been escalating to the owner again.
Exit
Hope runs out. A leadership restructuring produces my director's own diagnosis: “decision paralysis.” I stop waiting for change that isn't coming.

The Wireshark moment

I could explain away some of the problems. Maybe the laptop request was about budget. Maybe Kubernetes was a staffing issue. But the Wireshark thing made it clear this was bigger than that.

A QA engineer on my team asked for Wireshark, a basic network tool that other teams already use. It took three months of meetings with security, legal, and directors to get it approved. The process costs more than the tool.

It's easy to see the cost of saying yes. The cost of saying no is harder to spot. You lose people, morale drops, and work slows down.

Watching a teammate spend three months fighting for a tool others already had was demoralizing. It wasn't just a waste of time. It showed me the company didn't value our time, and that this wasn't something I could fix. It was baked into the culture.

Seven months for a chatbot

The AI tooling mess sticks with me. Directors spent seven months arguing about which AI tools we could use. This was while AI was moving faster than anything I'd seen in my career. Every week we waited, we fell further behind.

In the end, the owner just told IT to do it and ignore security concerns. We got a ChatGPT Enterprise subscription. No API, no Copilot, no code completion. The tool that could have changed how I worked was handed to us in the weakest form possible.

Seven months. The owner had to step in. All we got was an MVP chatbot.

That was the result. The worst part wasn't even the outcome. It was seeing the same thing happen again. I had leadership support for a better, more open development environment. Six months later, nothing had changed. It was about to get escalated to the owner again.

What I got wrong

I need to be honest about my part in this. Blaming the company for everything wouldn't be fair or accurate.

I didn't say no soon enough. I kept taking on problems that should have been escalated or just left alone. Kubernetes is the best example. I took on all the responsibility instead of making the company deal with the risk. My reliability just covered up the real issues. By the time I pushed for real change, it was too late.

If I could do it over, I'd set clear limits earlier. I'd call out the risks sooner. I'd ask better questions before taking on something important: Who else is responsible? What happens if things get stuck? Is leadership actually going to fix this, or just put it off?

I assumed those things would sort themselves out. They don't.

What I'd tell the next engineer in this situation

If you're reliable, the company might use that as an excuse not to fix things.
If you keep taking on single points of failure, you take away the pressure for the company to fix them. Sometimes the best thing you can do is refuse to be the fix for a broken system.
If decisions are always slow, that's not just a rough patch. It's a sign of how things work.
One slow approval is a process issue. Three in a row, across different projects and teams, means it's the culture. Pay attention to the pattern.
Getting buy-in isn't the same as making progress.
I got leadership to agree on a new dev environment, then watched nothing happen for six months. Getting everyone in a room to say yes is easy. What matters is what happens after.
Hope isn't a plan, for you or for the company.
I stayed through a leadership change because I hoped things would get better. They didn't. At some point, staying is its own decision, and you have to make it on purpose.

Leaving as a signal

I'm not bitter about it. I learned an enormous amount, technically, organizationally, and about myself. I know what I'd do differently, and I'm bringing a more deliberate version of myself to whatever comes next.

When a senior engineer who built and ran key systems leaves after trying to fix things, that's not neutral. It shows the gap between what a company says it values and what it actually does.

In my case, that gap was about three years wide.
