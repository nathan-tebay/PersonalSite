Career · Job hunt

Three interviews, three lessons — an honest account of the first months of job hunting after a 20-year software career.

Nathan Tebay · April 2026

It's been three months since I quit my last job. I have had three interviews; this post is to reflect on those. Where I know I should have done better, and the experience of the job hunt. I'm currently considering local retail work to make ends meet. It's disheartening that a 20-year career in software is going on pause due to the landscape and my inability to show my full value in the few interviews I have landed.

Immediately after my resignation, a former co-worker reached out with an opportunity I was excited about. A startup in a similar market to the one I was in, a growing progressive company in a remote role. While I was excited, I wasn't ready, and it seemed like they weren't either. My interview was with their VP of technology. I went through my work history; it seemed like it went well. I had a plan for a team interview the following week, but it never came. I reached out to the interviewer to hear back three weeks later through LinkedIn. They had hired a new hiring manager who was to reach out to me. Again, that interview never came, and I gave up, discouraged by the interaction.

I don't blame the person I was in touch with; I understand they are extremely busy with the company. I've seen company posts about them attending conferences, getting new partnerships, and generally being very busy. Things slip; I understand that to a point, but it still doesn't make me want to keep trying. If hiring isn't a high priority, supporting my work once hired likely won't be either. The reason I left my previous role.

For my part, I came into the original conversation holding too much baggage. Nerves still in overdrive from recent events, too descriptive about my experience with former employers, and why I left them. I was not making it easy to decide to continue the process. I know if I hadn't rushed, I would have been in a better place to have that conversation. I was still figuring out what I wanted and what it would take to get there.

My second interview didn't make it past the recruiter stage. It's a company that has a local office, has hired a couple of my former teammates, and I took extra time to make sure I had my ducks in a row before applying. I thought the interview went well, so I was somewhat surprised when the rejection email came a few days later. It was not a flat-out we're not interested, but we went with another candidate. Keep in touch. The recruiter connected on LinkedIn, so I believe that is the case, but it ultimately comes down to the current state of the job market. Large companies are laying off many great people, and they are competing directly with me. I don't have FAANG or startup experience; a lot of them do. There is a lot of competition, with jobs receiving 100s of qualified applicants and a very difficult task of sorting through them.

In my most recent interview, I reached the technical stage but didn't move forward. I own this one; the company provided ample time to complete the problem, having given a brief outline ahead of time. I chose to do it in Python, prepared as much as I could for a week, and still failed to complete the problem. The problem was matching data from an input set against a target with a depth requirement. I was prepared, wrote several rounds of sample code, created flash cards, and studied the wrong problem. I thought the data I was looking for would be deep in the data structure and messy. The actual data was clean, the same depth, with only a few null values to filter out. I got the matching down pretty quickly; what I realized too late was that the depth requirement meant rerunning matches against the entire set to a given depth. Still not terribly hard, but I went in with the wrong problem in my head and didn't shed that until it was too late. I tripped over known Python syntax, but I haven't used it much in the last couple of years as I've focused more on infrastructure at work. I had underestimated the effort I needed to prepare for the pressure of an interview.

That was only the second time I've had a coding interview. Working for smaller companies, you might have a take-home assignment but seldom live code. The first was 30 minutes: a 15-minute team conversation and a 15-minute LeetCode-style problem, not enough time for either. This most recent one was much better, but still a very uncertain process.

I've had brief periods of specialization over my career, but I am a generalist overall. I have broad experience, spread over decades, the person you go to for almost random asks. It's systems thinking that is more important than ever with AI adoption — however, it doesn't check the boxes on a job posting.

You look at a LinkedIn listing and see 1000+ applications in a few days for anything remote; it's very difficult to cut through that noise. Once you do, you get a targeted coding problem that filters you out based on a very narrow skill. I've been coding JavaScript since high school, but I don't use it regularly now. I spent 5 years doing full-stack .NET development in C# and VB.NET. I haven't touched those in 5 years. Wrote several Python back-end integrations over the course of a year, two years ago. I have the foundation to pick up and run with most anything in software and a large portion of IT, but I need a minute to put the pieces together.

These are skills that lend themselves to coding in the AI world, where stack consistency is not a given; you're no longer tied to deep knowledge of a language or framework to write code. Instead, you need to understand the solution you're asking AI to build, call BS when AI's solution is overly complex, and competently review AI's work regardless of the output language or framework. These are the fundamentals I see missing from coding exercises. Companies tout AI as a tool they support, but they don't trust it; they need to trust the human in control. They want to know how you think, which is important to that trust, yet you get judged by performance in a tiny, stressful window, working on shallow, targeted pieces of code.

Interviewing is a mess right now; getting an interview at all is a feat in itself, then begins the weeks- or months-long process of proving yourself through multiple interview rounds while paying the bills.

AI getting more competent adds to this as people question your value, a value that has shifted rapidly. Coding has become less a part of the job over the last few years. Knowing the how and why, explaining it concisely to AI and coworkers, and making great architectural decisions along the way are far more of the job now. I'm still figuring out the next chapter. The process is slower than expected, but I recognize my progress and continue to improve. I just need to keep working; I am getting better every day.
