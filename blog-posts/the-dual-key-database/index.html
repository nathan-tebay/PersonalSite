Database design · Technical debt

A story about the pitfalls of a database design where the main tables had dual keys based on a type identifier, and how one early decision compounded for fifteen years.

Nathan Tebay · March 2026

I worked on a system where one early database decision made life harder for everyone who touched it. The main table had an object_id as the primary key, a type field with five possible values, and separate id fields for each type. Depending on the type, different fields were required, and different tables were related. This broke a lot of the usual relational database rules. Foreign keys didn’t work the way you’d expect, so we had to handle a lot of operations in business logic. Even simple CRUD operations took more effort than they should have. Improving performance meant knowing all the quirks.

The main drawback of this design came from the ways it broke the relational database model. Database features such as cascading deletes and foreign keys were replaced with complex stored procedures or implemented in business logic, increasing maintenance overhead and making the phrase 'technical debt' all too common. Add to that multiple paths to interact with the database, a VB.NET-based BLL/DLL implementation for the main application, an API implemented as a POC for one section of the product, and multiple supporting products with their own implementations at different times, and implementing new features always came with a longer timeline than seemed reasonable. This single database design decision, while not wholly responsible for the technical debt, was a major contributor to it.

So why not fix it? How to fix it was often discussed amongst our small development team, and it was actually very doable at the database level.

The more challenging part was the 6+ month timeline for rewriting every flow that touched the database and the high risk of data corruption. These changes were not an option when new features in our multi-tenant application were important to keeping clients happy and revenue flowing.

It was a bit of a catch-22: stop new feature implementations at the risk of losing client revenue, or continue the way it had been for years, adding more to an already bloated application that had been growing for over a decade. Providing value to clients looking to get all they can out of a niche, expensive tool. The business needs to keep momentum, which trumps refactoring a functional, albeit sub-optimal, system design.

I’m not blaming anyone for how this design came to be. The original developer was figuring things out as they went, and the choices made sense at the time. They built a solid business over the years, and plenty of developers added their own fixes and workarounds as the product grew. My point is that a simple decision early on can have a big impact on the product, the team, and the company as time goes on.

Early design decisions compound — plan for the system you'll have in ten years, not the one you have today.
A database design that gets you to MVP can turn into a nightmare once the system grows. Cutting corners on relational constraints to save time early on just means every developer pays for it later, year after year.
